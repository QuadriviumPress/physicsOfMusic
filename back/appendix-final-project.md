The final project asks you to do two things that the problem sets cannot: to
measure something nobody handed you the answer to, and to explain what you found
to somebody who does not know any physics.

The second is the harder one, and it is the one most likely to matter to you
after the course ends.

## What the Project Is

Pick a question about musical sound that you can actually answer with a
measurement, make the measurement, and present the result in ten minutes to an
audience of your classmates, treated, for the purposes of the talk, as
intelligent people who have not taken this course.

That is the whole specification. The constraints hiding inside it are worth
making explicit.

**It must be a question, not a topic.** "The physics of the violin" is a topic,
and a talk on a topic is a summary of things other people found out. "Does
bowing closer to the bridge really add high harmonics, and how much?" is a
question. You can be wrong about a question, which is what makes answering one
worth ten minutes of an audience's attention.

**You must measure something yourself.** Not necessarily something new, almost
certainly not something new, but something you recorded, timed, counted, or
weighed. A project whose evidence is entirely other people's published figures
is a literature review, and it does not meet the outcome.

**The measurement must be possible with what you have.** A phone, a laptop, and
free software are enough for most of the topics below. If your question needs an
anechoic chamber, choose another question, or find the version of it that a
carpeted bedroom can answer.

## Choosing a Question

The reliable move is to take something you already know from experience and ask
*how much*. Three examples of the transformation:

| Experience | Question you can measure |
|---|---|
| "My guitar sounds brighter near the bridge." | How does the amplitude of the 5th harmonic vary with plucking position, and does the measured pattern match the prediction that a pluck at $L/n$ suppresses the $n$th harmonic? |
| "The stairwell makes my voice sound huge." | What is the stairwell's reverberation time, and how does it compare with my bedroom's? Does the Sabine equation predict it from the dimensions and surfaces? |
| "Cheap earbuds have no bass." | Where does the response of three pairs of headphones roll off at the low end, and by how much do they differ at 60 Hz? |

### Thirty starting points

**Instruments.** Plucking position and harmonic content · Why a wound bass
string works · The inharmonicity of a real piano string, measured · How much a
guitar's body resonance actually adds · What a mute does to a trumpet's spectrum
· The clarinet's missing even harmonics, verified · Where a recorder's register
hole has to go · The end correction of a real pipe · How a drum's pitch changes
with head tension · Why a struck bar sounds different from a plucked string

**Tuning and perception.** Is a real piano stretched, and by how much · How
close is a barbershop quartet to just intonation · How accurately can you tune a
fifth by beats · Your own frequency discrimination against frequency · The
missing fundamental, demonstrated on yourself · How many cents out does an
interval have to be before you notice · Whether the dissonance of an interval
changes with timbre

**Voice.** Formants of your own vowels, measured · How the vocal tract changes
between a spoken and a sung vowel · Whether you can find your own singer's
formant · How high a soprano can go before the vowel becomes unidentifiable

**Rooms and recording.** Reverberation time of three rooms · Room modes in a
small bedroom · What a polar pattern really looks like · How much a lossy codec
actually changes a waveform · Where aliasing becomes audible · Whether a
loudspeaker's frequency response matches its specification · How stereo
localization depends on delay versus level · What a hall's early reflections do
to clarity

## The Schedule

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Week 4 | Choose a question. Write it in one sentence. |
| Week 7 | **Proposal**: one page: the question, how you will measure it, what result you expect, and what would count as the measurement failing. |
| Week 11 | **Progress check**: show one real measurement, however rough. |
| Week 14 | Draft slides; practice the talk out loud, timed. |
| Week 15 | **Presentation**: ten minutes plus questions. |

The Week 7 proposal exists because the most common way this project goes wrong
is a question that cannot be measured, discovered in Week 13. Say in the
proposal what would count as failure; if you cannot name a result that would
disappoint you, the question probably has no content.

## Giving the Talk

Ten minutes is about 1,200 words. That is very little, and it is enough, if you
spend it on the right things.

**Spend the first minute on the question, not on background.** The audience does
not need the history of acoustics. They need to know what you wanted to find out
and why anyone would care. Play the sound if you have one, thirty seconds of a
guitar plucked in two places does more than a slide about harmonics.

**Show your own data, large.** One graph you made, with axes labeled in units a
non-specialist can read, beats six graphs from papers. If a number is the point
of the talk, put that number on the slide by itself.

**Say what you found, including if it was not what you expected.** A measurement
that disagreed with the prediction is a better talk than one that agreed, as long
as you say what you think went wrong. Agreement is only interesting if the
audience believed disagreement was possible.

**Translate every technical term or drop it.** You may use one or two, if you
define them in plain words the first time. "The spectrum: that is, a list of
which pure tones are present and how strong each one is" costs you four seconds
and keeps the room with you. An undefined "spectral centroid" loses it
permanently.

**Do not read the slides.** If a slide is readable in three seconds, it is doing
its job.

## What a Good Talk Sounds Like

Three tests, applied by the audience rather than by you:

- Can a listener state your question afterwards, in their own words?
- Can they say what you did to answer it?
- Do they believe the answer, and could they say why?

A talk that is technically flawless and fails all three has not met the
communication outcome. This is worth saying plainly because it is a genuinely
different skill from the one the problem sets develop, and it is the one that
transfers.

## Rubric

| | Weight | What is assessed |
|---|---|---|
| **The question** | 15% | Specific, answerable, and worth asking. Framed as something that could have come out otherwise. |
| **The measurement** | 30% | Your own data. Method described well enough to repeat. Uncertainty estimated, not ignored. |
| **The physics** | 25% | The result is connected to the physics of the course, correctly. A prediction is compared with the measurement and the comparison is honest. |
| **The explanation** | 25% | A non-specialist can follow it. Terms defined or avoided. Figures legible and labeled. Time respected. |
| **Response to questions** | 5% | Answers what was asked. Says "I don't know" where that is the true answer. |

Note the weighting: the explanation is worth as much as the physics. That is
deliberate, and it is the point of the exercise.

## A Note on Honesty

Report what you measured, including the run that did not work and the data point
you cannot explain. If your result disagrees with the textbook, say so and offer
a reason: a systematic error in your method is a perfectly respectable finding,
and identifying one is real physics. Quietly dropping the inconvenient half of
your data is the one thing in this project that cannot be recovered from.
